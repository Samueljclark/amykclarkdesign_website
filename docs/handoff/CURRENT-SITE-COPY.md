- **[H1]** How to read a fabric: weight, weave, and how it will actually hang
- **[P]** What weight, weave, and hand tell you about how a fabric will hang once it is made up, and what a memo sample will never show you.
- **[P]** July 8, 2026
- **[P]** Nearly everyone chooses fabric the same way. You find a color you love, hold the sample at arm’s length, and decide. Color is the one thing a sample tells you honestly. Almost everything else about how that fabric will behave once it is cut, sewn, and hung is invisible in a four-inch square.
- **[P]** Here is what Amy looks at instead.
- **[H2]** Weight tells you what it will do, not how good it is
- **[P]** Weight is measured in ounces per yard, and heavier is not better. It is only different. A heavy fabric holds a fold and stays where you put it. A light fabric moves, catches light, and will not hold a pleat on its own.
- **[P]** Which one is right depends on what your window has to do. A dining room you only use in the evening can carry weight. A breakfast room that lives on morning light usually should not.
- **[P]** The mistake she sees most is a light fabric chosen for a tall window because it looked airy on a table. At nine feet it reads limp. It does not drape, it hangs.
- **[H2]** Weave decides what the light does
- **[P]** Hold the sample up to a window before you do anything else. Watch how much light comes through and what happens to the color when it does.
- **[P]** A tight weave blocks light and keeps its color. An open weave glows, and the color shifts, sometimes a lot. Linen with a loose, irregular weave will read three shades lighter with the sun behind it than it did lying on the table.
- **[P]** Weave also predicts trouble. A loose weave in a sunny south-facing room will relax and grow over a season. A slubby, nubbly weave is beautiful in the hand and can fight a crisp pleat. Silk is glorious and will fade in direct sun, which is a lining decision rather than a reason to avoid it.
- **[H2]** Hand predicts the fold
- **[P]** Hand is how a fabric feels and how it moves when you gather it. It is the closest thing to a preview of the finished treatment, and it takes two seconds.
- **[P]** Take a good handful and squeeze it, then let go. Does it fall back open, or stay crushed? Does it want to fold in soft rolls or sharp creases? Does it spring back like upholstery or collapse like a sheet?
- **[P]** A fabric that resists being gathered will resist being pleated. A fabric that wrinkles in your fist will wrinkle across a chair seat.
- **[H2]** A pattern gathered is a different pattern
- **[P]** This is the one that surprises people most. A large pattern that reads beautifully flat can disappear entirely once it is pleated, because two-thirds of it ends up inside the folds. A stripe that looked bold flat becomes a thin, busy line. A big open floral becomes texture.
- **[P]** So the question is never whether you like this pattern. It is whether you like the part of it that will still be showing.
- **[P]** Where the pattern has to survive intact, the treatment changes to suit it: a flat roman shade, a flat panel, a valance, an upholstered seat. That decision gets made at the same time as the fabric, not afterwards.
- **[H2]** Almost anything can be made to behave
- **[P]** Interlining adds body and weight and makes a lighter fabric hang like a heavier one. Blackout belongs in bedrooms. A sun-facing room needs protection from fade regardless of what the face fabric is.
- **[P]** Which means the honest answer to “will this fabric work” is usually “it depends what goes behind it.”
- **[H2]** What the sample cannot tell you
- **[P]** A memo sample cannot tell you how a fabric looks after a season of being drawn every evening, how a seam sits once the pattern has to match across two panels, or whether the hem will hang level on a floor that is not level.
- **[P]** That part comes from having made the thing before. Amy chooses fabric standing in the room it is going into, at the hour the light matters most, and she chooses it for how it will behave at length, not for how it photographs in the hand.
- **[P]** If you are working out fabric for a specific window, that is what custom drapery is for. The fabric, the lining, the pleat, and the hardware all get decided together, by the person who will be sewing it. The same reading applies to upholstery, where hand and durability matter more than drape, and the wrong weight shows up in six months rather than on installation day.

- **[H1]** Drapery, shades, or blinds: how to decide room by room
- **[P]** A room-by-room way to choose between drapery, shades, and blinds, starting from what the window actually has to do rather than from the product.
- **[P]** July 16, 2026
- **[P]** Most people come to this question backwards. They decide they want roman shades, then work out which windows get them. Turn it around, because drapery, shades, and blinds are not three styles of the same thing. They solve different problems, and your room will tell you which problem you have.
- **[H2]** Ask what the window has to do
- **[P]** Four questions, in this order.
- **[P]** What is wrong with the room right now, the light, the privacy, the temperature, or the way it looks? What hour of the day does it bother you? Will anyone actually operate this every day, or does it need to be set once and left? And is the window itself worth showing, or is it something to cover up?
- **[P]** The answers usually narrow it to one option before any fabric comes out.
- **[H2]** Living rooms want both, in layers
- **[P]** A living room gets used across a whole day of moving light, which is the hardest brief of the four. It is also the room where the treatment does the most visual work.
- **[P]** The usual answer is layered. A shade or blind handles the light and the glare. Drapery at the sides handles the architecture. The shade does the work. The drapery makes the window look finished and the ceiling look higher, and much of the time it never closes at all.
- **[P]** If you only do one, do the drapery, and accept that you will squint at the television for one hour in the afternoon. If glare is genuinely the problem, do the shade first and add drapery later. That order works. The reverse is harder.
- **[H2]** Bedrooms are a darkness problem
- **[P]** Bedrooms are the one room with a right answer. You want it dark, and you want the light leaking around the edges dealt with.
- **[P]** Blackout lining handles the middle of the window. What it does not handle is the gap at the sides, and that is where nearly all the light in a supposedly dark bedroom is coming from. A return that closes tight against the frame, or a shade mounted inside the opening under the drapery, is what actually makes a room dark.
- **[P]** Street-facing bedrooms have a second requirement that gets confused with the first. Privacy and darkness are not the same thing. A top-down bottom-up shade covers the lower half of the window and leaves the daylight above it, which is usually exactly what you want during the day.
- **[H2]** Kitchens and baths: whatever wipes clean
- **[P]** Fabric near a stove or a shower is a maintenance decision more than a design one. Moisture, grease, and steam are unkind to anything you have to take down to clean.
- **[P]** A blind or a woven wood shade is usually the honest answer here, and a short valance can add softness without putting yards of fabric next to a cooktop. If the window is over a sink, whatever goes on it has to be reachable with wet hands.
- **[H2]** Windows nobody can reach
- **[P]** Tall windows, wide spans, anything above a stair or stranded behind furniture. Choose these for whether they will ever be adjusted, because most of the time they are not. A treatment that stays in one position forever might as well have been specified for that position.
- **[P]** Motorization solves it, and it is far easier to decide before drywall than after. It also lets a whole room drop on a schedule, ahead of the afternoon sun rather than after the room has cooked.
- **[H2]** When the window itself is the point
- **[P]** Some windows should not be covered. A leaded-glass panel, a good arch, a bay with a view worth having. The treatment should frame those, not hide them. Drapery held at the sides, or a shade that stacks up almost invisibly at the top, keeps the architecture and still gives you a way to shut the room down when you want to.
- **[P]** The opposite is also true. A window that is off-center, oddly proportioned, or too small for its wall can be corrected. Rod width, mount height, and where the panels stack are what do the correcting, and those get decided in the room with a tape measure.
- **[H2]** Where each one comes from
- **[P]** One practical difference worth knowing. Blinds and shades are manufactured products, specified and ordered to the measurements of your opening, including roman shades, which come from the manufacturer built to spec rather than out of Amy’s workroom. Drapery is made by hand for your window, which is why the fabric range is wide open and the details are all yours to decide.
- **[P]** That is why a room usually ends up with both, and why the two decisions are best made at the same time. The shade and the drapery have to agree about where the light is going.

- **[H1]** What actually makes a window treatment look expensive
- **[P]** Fullness, length, stack, lining, and how a panel is dressed on installation day. The details that separate finished work from covered windows.
- **[P]** July 24, 2026
- **[P]** You can walk into a room and know immediately. Nothing about it announces itself, and yet the windows read as finished rather than covered. It is almost never the fabric. It is five or six decisions, all made before anything was cut, and none of them fixable afterwards.
- **[H2]** Fullness, before anything else
- **[P]** Fullness is how much fabric is in the panel compared to the width it covers. It is the biggest tell, and it is the first thing cut when a treatment is being made down to a number.
- **[P]** A panel at two and a half times the width of its opening falls in deep, full columns. The same panel at one and a half times looks stretched, and no fabric is good enough to hide it. You can see it from across a room without knowing what you are looking at. The folds are shallow, the spacing is wide, and the whole thing reads thin.
- **[P]** There is no trick that substitutes for fullness. It is the yardage.
- **[H2]** Length, and the inch that matters
- **[P]** Drapery either reaches the floor or it does not, and there is very little middle ground that looks deliberate. A panel that stops an inch above the floor looks like it shrank. A panel that just kisses the floor, or puddles a little, looks like it was made for the room.
- **[P]** The same discipline applies at the top. Hanging the rod at the window frame makes your ceiling look lower. Hanging it high, close to the ceiling, and letting the panel run the full height makes the window look taller than it is. It is the simplest change available and almost nobody makes it.
- **[H2]** Where the panels go when they are open
- **[P]** Stack-back is how much wall the panels take up when they are pulled open, and it decides whether the treatment feels generous or feels in the way. A rod that stops at the frame means the open panels sit on the glass, blocking light and view for no reason. Extending the rod past the frame on both sides gives them somewhere to go.
- **[P]** That has to be decided before the hardware is ordered, because bracket projection and return determine how the finished treatment sits against the wall. A rod chosen for looks alone will bow under interlined panels, and a bowed rod is visible forever.
- **[H2]** Lining is what you are actually seeing
- **[P]** From the street, all anyone sees is the lining, which is a good reason not to treat it as an afterthought. From inside, the lining is what gives the face fabric its body.
- **[P]** Interlining is the layer most people have never heard of, and it is what makes expensive drapery feel expensive. It adds weight between the face fabric and the lining, so the panel hangs in soft, heavy folds and holds them. It is the difference between fabric that drapes and fabric that hangs.
- **[H2]** The details at the edges
- **[P]** Trim is where a treatment stops looking bought and starts looking made. A contrast band down a leading edge. A banded border on a roman shade that picks up something else in the room. A return that closes tight against the frame so no light sneaks around the side.
- **[P]** Then there is the work that is nearly impossible to photograph and obvious the second you walk in. The hem hangs level. The pattern matches across two panels and across every seam. The pleats are spaced evenly, and the spacing is the same on both sides of the window. Nothing draws attention, which is the whole point.
- **[H2]** It gets dressed on installation day
- **[P]** Here is the part that gets skipped most often. A brand-new panel does not know how to fold. Straight out of the workroom it hangs however it was packed, with the creases in the wrong places, and it will keep those creases for months if nobody trains it.
- **[P]** Dressing means going through every fold by hand on installation day, setting each one, and tying the panel loosely so it learns the fold. It takes a while, and it is the difference between drapery that looks right on day one and drapery that looks right eventually, if at all.
- **[P]** Amy installs what she makes, which means the person who decided how the panels should fall is the one standing there setting them.
- **[P]** If you want to see how these decisions get made in a specific room, that is what custom drapery covers. Fabric, lining, pleat, hardware, and the dressing, all decided together. For pillows and cushions, the same principle applies at a smaller scale. Fill, seam, and welt do exactly what fullness and lining do on a window.
