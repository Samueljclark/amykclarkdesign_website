## Services — Custom Drapery

**Page title:** Custom Drapery Cincinnati | Amy K Clark Design

**Meta description:** Custom drapery and curtains for Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky homes. Fabric selection, fabrication, and installation by Amy K Clark Design.

Services

**H1:** Custom Drapery

Custom drapery in Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky, worked out in the room it belongs to. Amy picks the fabric, settles the details, sews the treatment, and hangs it herself. The custom curtains that show up on installation day are the ones the room was measured for.

**H2:** Start in the room

Drapery either works with a room's architecture or argues with it. Where the rod goes. How far the panels stack back when they are open, and how much wall they take up doing it. Those calls are what make a room read taller and finished instead of merely covered.

Amy works it out standing in the room, at the hour the light matters most. A north-facing sitting room and a west-facing dining room want different weights and different linings even when the windows are identical.

**H2:** What the fabric will actually do

Fabric comes from Schumacher and the other lines I represent, chosen for behavior rather than for how it photographs on a memo sample. How it hangs. What it looks like after a season of being drawn every evening. A linen that is gorgeous flat can fall like a wet towel at length, and a pattern that reads beautifully in the hand can vanish the moment it is gathered.

Lining is settled in the same conversation. Interlining adds weight and body and makes a lighter fabric hang like a heavier one. Blackout belongs in bedrooms and media rooms. Sun-facing rooms need protection from fade. These are the choices that decide whether a treatment still looks right in five years.

**H2:** Pleats and hardware

Pleat style sets the character. Euro pleats read cleaner and more current. French pleats hold a deeper fold and lean traditional. Ripplefold moves as one continuous wave, which suits a wide contemporary span. Goblet pleats belong in the rooms that can carry them.

Hardware gets chosen alongside the fabric, never after. Bracket projection and return decide how the finished treatment sits against the wall, and a rod picked for looks alone will bow under interlined panels. Trim is where a treatment stops looking bought and starts looking made. A contrast band down a leading edge. A return that closes tight to the frame so no light sneaks around the side.

**H2:** A decorator does not make anything

Amy sews the drapery herself and installs it herself. Nothing goes out to a workroom that never saw the room, and nobody hangs it who was not part of the decisions. Panels are dressed and trained on installation day so the folds fall correctly from the start, and the treatment is left looking the way it should look every day after.

Hard to photograph, obvious the second you walk in: the hem hangs level, the pattern matches across panels, the stack clears the glass. Precise work, done by someone who knew what the room needed before anything was cut.

Services

**H1:** Blinds & Shades

Custom blinds in Cincinnati and custom shades across Northern Kentucky, specified for the way a room actually gets used. Lafayette Interior Fashions is the one line I represent in this category, so every Lafayette blind and shade here comes from a single catalog. One manufacturer, measured and installed by the person who helped choose it.

**H2:** One line, on purpose

Amy works from the Lafayette catalog rather than assembling treatments out of whichever supplier happens to be convenient that week. Blinds, shades, shutters, woven woods. Knowing one catalog cold produces better specifications than knowing six of them halfway.

Roman shades live here as a manufactured product, built to Lafayette's specification rather than in Amy's workroom. When a room wants a fabric Roman shade made to order, a particular textile with a particular band, that is workroom work and it gets handled as custom drapery instead.

**H2:** Decide these before anything is ordered

Most regret about blinds and shades traces back to a decision nobody actually made. A shade picked for how it looked in a showroom, not for what the window does at four in the afternoon.

**BUTTON:** Light and glare control

How much light a room should keep is a different question from how much it lets in. A west-facing room with a television needs glare handled at one specific hour. A kitchen usually wants every bit of daylight it can hold. Solar shades are rated by openness, and that number decides how much view survives when they are down.

**BUTTON:** Privacy

Privacy and light are not the same requirement, and treating them as one is the most common mistake here. A top-down/bottom-up shade covers the lower half of a window and leaves the daylight coming in above it, which is usually what a street-facing bedroom or bathroom actually needs. Sheers give daytime privacy and then read completely differently after dark, once the lamps are on inside.

**BUTTON:** Motorization

Motorization earns its keep on tall windows, wide spans, and anything stranded above a stair or behind furniture, the treatments nobody ever adjusts because reaching them is a nuisance. It also lets a whole room drop on a schedule, ahead of the afternoon sun instead of after the room has already cooked. Battery, hardwired, and solar-charged suit different constructions, and the decision is far easier before drywall than after.

**BUTTON:** Child and pet safety

Cordless, in any house with young children or pets. There is rarely a good reason to choose otherwise. Cordless lift, motorization, and tensioned continuous-loop mounts all take the hazard of a traditional pull cord off the table. Worth deciding on purpose rather than by omission.

**BUTTON:** Room by room

Bathrooms and kitchens need something that shrugs off moisture and wipes clean. Bedrooms usually want room-darkening or blackout, often layered under drapery. Living rooms need something adjustable enough for a whole day of moving light. Woven woods warm up a room that reads flat. Shutters suit a window where the treatment should read as architecture rather than fabric.

**H2:** An eighth of an inch

Blinds and shades are unforgiving about measurement in a way drapery is not. An eighth of an inch decides whether an inside mount clears the frame or binds against it, and a mount depth that looked fine on paper can prove otherwise once the hardware is in hand. Amy measures every opening herself and installs the finished product herself.

Windows in older Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky houses are almost never square. The allowances for that are a judgment call rather than a formula, and making them correctly is what keeps a treatment from looking almost right.

## Services — Upholstery

**Page title:** Custom Upholstery Cincinnati | Amy K Clark Design

**Meta description:** Custom upholstery and reupholstery for Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky homes. Fabric selection and finished detail by Amy K Clark Design.

Services

**H1:** Upholstery

Custom upholstery in Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky, for furniture worth keeping. Amy helps decide what a piece should become, picks the fabric for how it will wear, and handles the details that separate a reupholstered chair from a chair with new fabric on it.

**H2:** Start with the frame

Not every chair should be reupholstered, and saying so out loud is part of the job. The frame decides it. Hardwood, joined and doweled instead of stapled, will take new fabric almost regardless of age, and eight-way hand-tied springs are worth keeping. Good bones under an unfashionable fabric is the ideal candidate. A failing frame on a particleboard base usually is not. A loose joint can be reglued; a cracked rail is a different conversation, and that is better known before anything comes apart.

Sentiment counts, and it counts legitimately. A chair from a grandparent's house gets done properly because it is that chair, and no other reason is required. That decision belongs to the client, made with clear information rather than being talked into or out of it.

**H2:** Fabric that survives the room

Upholstery fabric answers to different demands than drapery. It gets sat on, leaned against, spilled on, and cleaned. Double rubs measure abrasion resistance, and the number that suits a formal dining chair is nowhere near what a family room sectional needs. Performance fabrics have improved enough to choose on their own merits now, not as a concession to children and dogs.

Pattern scale matters more here than almost anywhere, because the fabric is being fitted to a three-dimensional object. A large repeat can be beautiful on a sofa back and unusable on a tight-seat chair. Railroading, whether a pattern runs along the bolt or across it, decides whether a wide piece can be covered without a seam down the middle. All of that is settled before yardage is ordered.

**H2:** The details that show

Welt does more to the character of a finished piece than most people expect. Self-welt keeps it quiet. Contrast welt gives it an edge and draws the shape out. A tailored skirt reads nothing like a waterfall, and an exposed leg reads differently again. Cushion fill decides how the piece feels a year in: down-wrapped foam where softness matters, something firmer where people sit every single day.

Pattern placement is where the care becomes visible. A motif centered on the seat and centered again on the back. Stripes running true down an arm. These are decisions made by someone standing over the piece, not by a machine following a cutting order.

Custom pillows and cushions in Cincinnati and Northern Kentucky, the layer that finishes a room. These are the smallest things Amy makes and often the ones that decide whether a room reads as considered or merely furnished.

**H2:** Small work, disproportionate effect

A ready-made pillow is one size, one fabric, one edge, all decided by somebody who never saw the sofa. Custom means the proportion suits the piece it sits on and the finish is chosen rather than accepted. It is also the easiest way to get a remarkable textile into a room. A fabric that would be impractical across a whole sofa is perfect as a pair of pillows, and a size that does not exist ready-made is not an obstacle when Amy is making it anyway.

This is often where a project starts. A client who is not ready to commit to drapery can see exactly how Amy works from a set of pillows, and the room moves further than the size of the job suggests it should.

**H2:** Proportion and fill

Size is the thing people get wrong most. Pillows that are too small leave a sofa looking undressed. A mix of sizes with a deliberate relationship between them beats a matched set of four, and two large pillows can carry a long sofa better than four small ones ever will. Seat depth and back height decide what belongs there, along with whether anyone actually sits on it.

Fill decides how a pillow behaves. Down and down-blend inserts have a hand that foam cannot fake, and they take the karate chop, or the casual slump that makes a room look lived in rather than staged. Inserts are sized larger than the cover so the pillow sits full instead of slack. Small detail, immediately visible.

The edge sets the register. A knife edge is clean and quiet. A flange softens it and reads traditional. Box edge where the pillow needs to hold structure. Brush fringe and tape push it formal fast, and there is a line past which a pillow is wearing a costume.

**H2:** Cushions and the rest

Window seats, benches, and banquettes need cushions built to an actual measurement rather than trimmed down to fit. Foam density gets chosen for how long people sit there, with a boxed construction that holds its front edge instead of collapsing into it. Dining chair seats take the most abuse in the house and get built accordingly. Outdoor cushions have to survive weather and then dry out properly.

Past pillows and cushions, soft furnishings covers table linens, bed skirts, shams, headboards, and the occasional piece that has no standard name and simply has to be invented for the space it goes in.

## Journal — How to Read a Fabric

**Page title:** How to Read a Fabric | Amy K Clark Design

**Meta description:** What weight, weave, and hand tell you about how a fabric will hang once it is made up, and what a memo sample will never show you.

Journal

**H1:** How to read a fabric: weight, weave, and how it will actually hang

July 8, 2026

Nearly everyone chooses fabric the same way. They find a color they love, hold the sample at arm’s length, and decide. The color is the one thing a sample tells you honestly. Almost everything else about how that fabric will behave once it is cut, sewn, and hung is invisible in a four-inch square.

Here is what to look at instead.

**H2:** Weight tells you what it will do, not how good it is

Weight is measured in ounces per yard, and heavier is not better. It is only different. A heavy fabric holds a fold. It falls in deep, deliberate columns and stays where it is put. A light fabric moves, catches light, and will not hold a pleat on its own.

Which one is right depends on what the window has to do. A dining room that only gets used in the evening can carry weight. A breakfast room that lives on morning light usually should not.

The mistake is choosing a light fabric for a tall window because it looked airy on a table. At nine feet, an underweight fabric reads limp. It does not drape; it hangs.

**H2:** Weave decides what the light does

Hold a sample up to a window before anything else. Watch how much light comes through and what happens to the color when it does.

A tight weave blocks light and keeps its color under it. An open weave glows, and the color shifts — sometimes considerably. Linen with a loose, irregular weave will read three shades lighter with the sun behind it than it did lying on the table.

Weave also predicts trouble. A loose weave in a sunny south-facing room will relax and grow over a season. A slubby, nubbly weave is beautiful in the hand and can fight a crisp pleat. Silk is glorious and will fade in direct sun, which is a lining decision rather than a reason to avoid it.

**H2:** Hand predicts the fold

Hand is how a fabric feels and how it moves when you gather it. It is the closest thing to a preview of the finished treatment, and it takes two seconds.

Take a good handful of the sample and squeeze it, then let it go. Does it fall back open, or does it stay crushed? Does it want to fold in soft rolls or sharp creases? Does it spring back like upholstery or collapse like a sheet?

A fabric that resists being gathered will resist being pleated. A fabric that wrinkles in your fist will wrinkle across a chair seat.

**H2:** A pattern gathered is a different pattern

This is the one that surprises people most. A large pattern that reads beautifully flat can disappear entirely once it is pleated, because two-thirds of it ends up inside the folds. A stripe that looked bold flat becomes a thin, busy line. A big open floral becomes texture.

So the question is never “do I like this pattern.” It is “do I like the part of this pattern that will still be showing.”

Where the pattern must survive intact, the treatment has to change to suit it — a flat Roman shade, a flat panel, a valance, an upholstered seat. That is a design decision that gets made at the same time as the fabric, not afterwards.

**H2:** Weight is also a lining decision

Almost every fabric can be made to behave. Interlining adds body and weight and makes a lighter fabric hang like a heavier one. Blackout belongs in bedrooms and media rooms. A sun-facing room needs protection from fade regardless of what the face fabric is.

Which means the honest answer to “will this fabric work” is usually “it depends what we put behind it.”

**H2:** What the sample cannot tell you

A memo sample cannot tell you how a fabric looks after a season of being drawn every evening, how a seam sits once the pattern has to match across two panels, or whether the hem will hang level on a floor that is not level.

That is the part that comes from having made the thing before. I choose fabric standing in the room it is going into, at the hour the light matters most, and I choose it for how it will behave at length — not for how it photographs in the hand.

If you are working out fabric for a specific window, that is what custom drapery is for: the fabric, the lining, the pleat, and the hardware all decided together, by the person who will be sewing it. The same reading applies to upholstery , where hand and durability matter more than drape, and the wrong weight shows up in six months rather than on installation day.

## Journal — Drapery, Shades, or Blinds

**Page title:** Drapery, Shades, or Blinds? | Amy K Clark Design

**Meta description:** A room-by-room way to choose between drapery, shades, and blinds, starting from what the window actually has to do rather than from the product.

Journal

**H1:** Drapery, shades, or blinds: how to decide room by room

July 16, 2026

Most people come to this question backwards. They decide they want Roman shades, and then work out which windows get them. It is worth turning around, because drapery, shades, and blinds are not three styles of the same thing. They solve different problems, and the room will tell you which problem you have.

**H2:** Ask what the window has to do

Before anything else, four questions, in this order.

What is wrong with this room right now — is it the light, the privacy, the temperature, or the way it looks? At what hour of the day does it bother you? Will anyone actually operate this treatment every day, or does it need to be set once and left? And is the window itself worth showing, or is it something to cover up?

The answers usually narrow it to one option before any fabric comes out.

**H2:** Living rooms want both, in layers

A living room is used across a whole day of moving light, which is the hardest brief of the four. It is also the room where the treatment does the most visual work.

The usual answer is layered: a shade or blind that handles the light and glare, and drapery at the sides that handles the architecture. The shade does the work. The drapery makes the window look finished and the ceiling look higher, and much of the time it never closes at all.

If you only do one, do the drapery, and accept that you will squint at the television for one hour in the afternoon. If the glare is genuinely the problem, do the shade first and add drapery later — that order works; the reverse is harder.

**H2:** Bedrooms are a darkness problem

Bedrooms are the one room where there is a right answer. You want it dark, and you want the light that leaks around the edges dealt with.

Blackout lining in drapery handles the middle of the window. What it does not handle is the gap at the sides, and that is where nearly all of the light in a supposedly dark bedroom is coming from. A return that closes tight against the frame, or a shade mounted inside the opening under the drapery, is what actually makes a room dark.

Street-facing bedrooms have a second requirement that gets confused with the first. Privacy and darkness are not the same thing. A top-down/bottom-up shade covers the lower half of the window and leaves the daylight above it, which is usually exactly what a bedroom on a street wants during the day.

**H2:** Kitchens and baths: whatever wipes clean

Fabric near a stove or a shower is a maintenance decision more than a design one. Moisture, grease, and steam are unkind to anything that has to be taken down to be cleaned.

This is where a blind or a woven wood shade is usually the honest answer, and where a short valance can add the softness without putting yards of fabric next to a cooktop. If a window is over a sink, whatever goes on it has to be reachable with wet hands.

**H2:** Windows nobody can reach

Tall windows, wide spans, anything above a stair or stranded behind furniture: these get chosen for whether they will ever be adjusted. Most of the time they are not, because reaching them is a nuisance, and a treatment that stays in one position forever might as well have been specified for that position.

Motorization solves it, and it is far easier to decide before drywall than after. It also lets a whole room drop on a schedule, ahead of the afternoon sun rather than after the room has already cooked.

**H2:** When the window itself is the point

Some windows should not be covered. A leaded-glass panel, a good arch, a bay with a view worth having — the treatment should frame those, not hide them. Drapery held at the sides, or a shade that stacks up almost invisibly at the top, keeps the architecture and still gives you a way to shut the room down when you want to.

Conversely, a window that is off-center, oddly proportioned, or too small for its wall can be corrected. Rod width, mount height, and where the panels stack are what do the correcting, and they are decisions that get made in the room with a tape measure.

**H2:** Where each one comes from

There is one practical difference worth knowing. Blinds and shades are manufactured products, specified and ordered to the measurements of your opening — including Roman shades, which come from the manufacturer built to spec rather than out of my workroom. Drapery is made, by hand, for your window, which is why the fabric range is wide open and the details are all yours to decide.

That is why a room usually ends up with both, and why the two decisions are best made at the same time. The shade and the drapery have to agree about where the light is going.

## Journal — What Makes a Window Treatment Look Expensive

**Page title:** Why Drapery Looks Expensive | Amy K Clark Design

**Meta description:** Fullness, length, stack, lining, and how a panel is dressed on installation day. The details that separate finished work from covered windows.

Journal

**H1:** What actually makes a window treatment look expensive

July 24, 2026

You can walk into a room and know immediately. Nothing about it announces itself, and yet the windows read as finished rather than covered. It is almost never the fabric. It is five or six decisions, each of them made before anything was cut, and none of them fixable afterwards.

**H2:** Fullness, before anything else

Fullness is how much fabric is in the panel compared to the width it covers. It is the single biggest tell, and it is the first thing cut when a treatment is being made down to a number.

A panel at two and a half times the width of its opening falls in deep, full columns. The same panel at one and a half times looks stretched, and no fabric is good enough to hide it. You can see it from across a room without knowing what you are looking at — the folds are shallow, the spacing is wide, and the whole thing reads thin.

There is no trick that substitutes for fullness. It is the yardage.

**H2:** Length, and the inch that matters

Drapery either reaches the floor or it does not, and there is very little middle ground that looks deliberate. A panel that stops an inch above the floor looks like it shrank. A panel that breaks slightly, just kissing the floor or puddling a little, looks like it was made for the room.

The same discipline applies at the top. Hanging the rod at the window frame makes the ceiling look lower. Hanging it high, close to the ceiling, and letting the panel run the full height makes the window look taller than it is. It is the simplest change available and almost nobody makes it.

**H2:** Where the panels go when they are open

Stack-back is how much wall the panels take up when they are pulled open, and it decides whether the treatment feels generous or feels in the way. A rod that stops at the frame means open panels sit on the glass, blocking light and view for no reason. Extending the rod past the frame on both sides gives the panels somewhere to go.

That decision has to be made before the hardware is ordered, because bracket projection and return are what determine how the finished treatment sits against the wall. A rod chosen for looks alone will bow under interlined panels, and a bowed rod is visible forever.

**H2:** Lining is what you are actually seeing

From the street, all anyone sees is the lining, which is a good reason not to treat it as an afterthought. From inside, the lining is what gives the face fabric its body.

Interlining is the layer most people have never heard of and it is what makes expensive drapery feel expensive. It adds weight between the face fabric and the lining, so the panel hangs in soft, heavy folds and holds them. It is the difference between fabric that drapes and fabric that hangs.

**H2:** The details at the edges

Trim is where a treatment stops looking bought and starts looking made. A contrast band down a leading edge. A banded border on a Roman shade that picks up something else in the room. A return that closes tight against the frame so no light sneaks around the side.

Then there is the work that is nearly impossible to photograph and obvious the second you walk in. The hem hangs level. The pattern matches across two panels and across every seam. The pleats are spaced evenly and the spacing is the same on both sides of the window. Nothing draws attention, which is the whole point.

**H2:** It gets dressed on installation day

Here is the part that gets skipped most often. A brand-new panel does not know how to fold. Straight out of the workroom it will hang however it was packed, with the creases in the wrong places, and it will keep those creases for months if nobody trains it.

Dressing means going through every fold by hand on installation day, setting each one, and tying the panel loosely so it learns the fold. It takes a while and it is the difference between drapery that looks right on day one and drapery that looks right eventually, if at all.

I install what I make, which means the person who decided how the panels should fall is the person standing there setting them. That is not a small thing. An interior decorator does not make anything, and the workroom that made it usually never saw the room.

If you want to see how these decisions get made in a specific room, that is what custom drapery covers — fabric, lining, pleat, hardware, and the dressing on installation day, all decided together. For pillows and cushions , the same principle applies at a smaller scale: fill, seam, and welt do exactly what fullness and lining do on a window.
